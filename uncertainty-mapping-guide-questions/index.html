Map your uncertainties in 10 minutes

Four prompts take you from messy notes to a one-page map built around the questions you want other fellows’ help with. Copy each prompt into Claude, fill in the highlighted parts, and keep going in the same chat.

010 min

Before you start

Have your raw material open: recent meeting notes, a project doc, or a two-minute brain dump. Messy is fine.

1. Let Claude read and reflect

2 min

Claude restates your project before organizing anything. Its wrong guesses preview what an outsider would get wrong.

Prompt 1
I’m going to paste raw notes about a research project I’m working on. Don’t organize anything yet. Do two things:

1. Restate what you think my project is in three plain sentences, and list the assumptions you’re making about it.
2. List every uncertainty you can find, each phrased as a question: open questions, decisions not yet made, answers we’re waiting on, risks, and things we don’t yet know how to do. Group them by type.

My notes:
[PASTE YOUR NOTES HERE]

Look for: assumptions Claude got wrong. You’ll correct them in the next step.

2. Correct it and draft the questions

2 min

This sets the frame (goal, tests, inputs) and turns each uncertainty into a card that leads with its open question. Claude also suggests which questions to put to the room. No visual yet.

Prompt 2
Corrections to your understanding:
[FIX ANY WRONG ASSUMPTIONS, A LINE OR TWO EACH. IF IT GOT EVERYTHING RIGHT, SAY SO.]

Now organize the uncertainties using the frame below. Don’t create the visual yet: write the content as plain text in your reply so I can check it. We’ll build the visual in the next step.

Frame:
- Goal: one sentence on what the project is trying to achieve.
- Tests: the one to three conditions the work must pass for the goal to count as met.
- Inputs: the uncertainties that feed those tests.
Show which uncertainties depend on which.

For each uncertainty, write a card that leads with its open question: the specific thing we don’t know, phrased so someone outside my field could answer it or offer a view. Not a topic label like “Data quality?”. Under the question, add three short parts: Known (one line of context), Resolved by, Blocks. Tag each card with how it gets resolved: Ask (waiting on someone else’s answer), Investigate (digging into evidence we have), Decide (making a choice), or Experiment (running something to see).

Audience: smart researchers from other fields who have no context on my project and will see this once while I talk them through it. Use plain language, add a short list defining any unavoidable jargon, keep each card under 60 words, and aim for four to six cards. Merge or drop detail an outsider wouldn’t need.

Finally, suggest the two or three questions where input from that audience would help me most. For each, say what kind of input would help: a similar case they’ve seen, a method, a contact, or a judgment call.

Look for: questions you genuinely don’t know the answer to. If a card’s question is really a topic heading, ask Claude to sharpen it before moving on.

3. Build the one-pager

3 min

Turns the cards into a page you can show, edit and share, with the open questions as the first thing anyone reads.

Prompt 3
Questions to put to the room:
[PICK TWO OR THREE FROM YOUR SUGGESTIONS, OR SWAP IN YOUR OWN]

Build this as a one-page visual I can present and share.

Layout: read left to right, the goal first, then the tests, then the open questions, with arrows pointing from each item to what it depends on and a short label on any arrow that needs one. Across the top: a one-sentence orientation, with any jargon defined in passing, and a colour key for Ask, Investigate, Decide and Experiment. Show each card’s tag as text as well as colour.

Make the open questions the most prominent text on the page. Each card’s headline is its open question, in the largest card text. Beneath it, one short line of context (what we know) in smaller, quieter text. Along the bottom, add a “Questions for the room” strip with the questions above, each numbered to match its card, with a few words on what kind of input would help. Mark those cards so they stand out on the map.

Prioritize information density: someone should take in the whole map in under a minute. Keep each card under 25 words and arrow labels to a few words. The full card text stays in this chat as my speaker notes.

Keep it to one page, and leave out anything confidential I haven’t cleared to share.

Look for: cover the context lines with your hand. Do the questions alone tell the story? And would a stranger know what you’re asking them?

4. Fix what seeing it reveals

3 min

A draft often surfaces something your notes never mentioned, such as a north-star question, a missing dependency, or a card that’s too detailed.

Prompt 4
Looking at the draft, here’s what’s missing or wrong:
[WHAT DID YOU ONLY NOTICE BY SEEING IT?]

Update the visual. Then check every open question: could someone outside my field answer it or offer a view? Rewrite any that only I could answer, and tell me which question an outsider is most likely to misread, and why.

Look for: the questions Claude rewrote or flagged. Read those aloud word for word when you present.

When you present

Walk the map left to right in under a minute: the goal, what must be true for it to count, then what you don’t know yet. Then point to “Questions for the room” and stay there. Read each question aloud, say what kind of input would help, and let people answer before you move to the next one. The map is the setup; the questions are the conversation.
