Map your uncertainties in 10 minutes

Four prompts take you from messy notes to a one-page visual you can talk a fellow through. Copy each prompt into Claude, fill in the highlighted parts, and keep going in the same chat.

1. Read2 min 2. Frame2 min 3. Build3 min 4. Fix3 min
010 min

Before you start

Have your raw material open: recent meeting notes, a project doc, or a two-minute brain dump. Messy is fine.

1. Let Claude read and reflect

2 min

Claude restates your project before organizing anything. Its wrong guesses preview what an outsider would get wrong.

Prompt 1
I’m going to paste raw notes about a research project I’m working on. Don’t organize anything yet. Do two things:

1. Restate what you think my project is in three plain sentences, and list the assumptions you’re making about it.
2. List every uncertainty you can find: open questions, decisions not yet made, answers we’re waiting on, risks, and things we don’t yet know how to do. Group them by type.

My notes:
[PASTE YOUR NOTES HERE]

Look for: assumptions Claude got wrong. You’ll correct them in the next step.

2. Correct it and draft the cards

2 min

This sets the frame (goal, tests, inputs) and drafts every card as text for an audience with no context. No visual yet.

Prompt 2
Corrections to your understanding:
[FIX ANY WRONG ASSUMPTIONS, A LINE OR TWO EACH. IF IT GOT EVERYTHING RIGHT, SAY SO.]

Now organize the uncertainties using the frame below. Don’t create the visual yet: write the content as plain text in your reply so I can check it. We’ll build the visual in the next step.

Frame:
- Goal: one sentence on what the project is trying to achieve.
- Tests: the one to three conditions the work must pass for the goal to count as met.
- Inputs: the uncertainties that feed those tests.
Show which uncertainties depend on which.

For each uncertainty, write a card with four parts: Known, Unknown, Resolved by, Blocks. Tag each card with how it gets resolved: Ask (waiting on someone else’s answer), Investigate (digging into evidence we have), Decide (making a choice), or Experiment (running something to see).

Audience: smart researchers from other fields who have no context on my project and will see this once while I talk them through it. Use plain language, add a short list defining any unavoidable jargon, keep each card under 60 words, and aim for four to six cards. Merge or drop detail an outsider wouldn’t need.

Look for: a goal you’d actually say out loud, and cards that a stranger could follow.

3. Build the one-pager

3 min

Turns the cards into something you can show, edit and share.

Prompt 3
Build this as a one-page visual I can present and share.

Layout: inputs on the left, tests in the middle, the goal on the right, with arrows showing dependencies and a short label on any arrow that needs one. Across the top: a two-sentence orientation, the terms list, and a colour key for Ask, Investigate, Decide and Experiment. Show each card’s tag as text as well as colour.

Keep it to one page, and leave out anything confidential I haven’t cleared to share.

Look for: whether the arrows tell the story. Someone should see what blocks what without reading every card.

4. Fix what seeing it reveals

3 min

A draft often surfaces something your notes never mentioned, such as a north-star question, a missing dependency, or a card that’s too detailed.

Prompt 4
Looking at the draft, here’s what’s missing or wrong:
[WHAT DID YOU ONLY NOTICE BY SEEING IT?]

Update the visual. Then tell me which single card an outsider is most likely to misunderstand, and why.

Look for: the card Claude flags. That’s where to slow down when you present.
